Ch. 4 §4.2 — stationary traffic states on I-25 NB (CDOT station 000501, 672 hourly obs, Aug 2026) — drag the model parameters and watch R² ? Daganzo §4.2.1: if traffic is stationary, its states (q, k, v) lie on one curve — a property of the road, not of the traffic. k (or v) parameterizes the curve; q cannot, because every flow occurs at two speeds. The upper branch is unrestricted flow, the lower is queued (restricted) — caused from downstream.

Each dot is one real hour on I-25 NB south of 6th Ave, Denver. Density is derived as k = q/u. Blue = unrestricted hours, red = queued (queue discharge) — note how they form the two branches of every diagram.

Graphical readings (Fig. 4.4): on v–k, flow = area of the rectangle to the origin; on q–k, speed = slope of the ray from the origin (and the curve's own slope w is a wave speed); on v–q, 1/k = slope of the ray.

Greenshields (1935): linear u = uf(1 − k/kj) → parabolic q-k. Capacity = ufkj/4 at k = kj/2.
Greenberg (1959): u = uc·ln(kj/k) — good in congestion, blows up (u→∞) as k→0.
Underwood (1961): u = uf·e−k/kc — good in free flow, never reaches jam.

Postulate (§4.2.1): stationary states lie on one curve, a property of the road itself. Assume the simplest v–k relation — a straight line between the two observable anchors, free-flow speed uf and jam density kj: v(k) = uf·(1 − k/kj) = —  (Eq. 4.19) Now apply the identity that Edie's definitions make exact for stationary regions (§4.1): q = k·v. The ▲ markers on all three charts are this capacity point.
Reality check — why Greenshields is "of historical interest"
The parabola is forced to be symmetric: capacity must sit exactly at k₀/kj = 1/2. Daganzo notes real freeways have k₀/kj ≈ 1/6 — flow rises steeply to capacity, then falls off gradually. You can see it in the I-25 dots on the q–k chart: the apex sits near k ≈ 150–190 veh/mi, while fitting the queued branch pushes kj toward 450+ — a ratio near 1/3, not 1/2. Drag uf and kj: you can match either the apex or the queued tail, never both. That tension is the whole argument for asymmetric shapes (and for the triangular diagram used in traffic dynamics, §4.3–4.4).

Two more representations complete Fig. 4.4: pace vs. flow (p = 1/v — the ancestor of Chapter 3's volume-delay functions, which keep only the unrestricted branch and ignore the queued one — a known source of trouble in congested networks) and speed vs. spacing (s = 1/k — the car-following view from Chapter 1, where capacity is the slope of the tangent ray from the origin).
