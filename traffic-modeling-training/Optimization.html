Travel time on the sample link vs v/c — hover to compare functions
iVolume-delay functions map a link's volume/capacity ratio to congested travel time — they are what UE/SUE assignment iterates on. BPR (1964) is the planning workhorse: cheap, flexible via α,β (defaults 0.15, 4; see NCHRP 365) — best for uninterrupted freeway/highway links. Akcelik (HCM 2000) is time-dependent: the analysis-period length T lets it stay finite and meaningful when demand exceeds capacity (X>1) — good for oversaturated corridors and arterials with control delay D₀. Combined link+node (Aashtiani et al.) adds Webster-type signalized and unsignalized intersection delay to a BPR link time — for urban networks where node delay dominates (shown here with a representative Webster form; TransModeler's exact k₁…k₄ formulation follows the same shape). Conical (Spiess 1990) mimics BPR but with bounded derivative and strictly increasing form — numerically better behaved in equilibrium algorithms than high-β BPR. Generalized cost adds tolls and operating cost via a value of time — for toll roads and multi-criteria routing. Logit VDF (Israel ITPR, representative sigmoid) caps runaway times with an S-shape including intersection delay.

iO-D Matrix Estimation (Nielsen's bi-level method, as in TransModeler): iterate between a traffic assignment (here UE between the two middle routes, BPR times) and a matrix adjustment that nudges O-D flows toward consistency with the link counts. Single Path compares volumes to counts only along each pair's current shortest path; Multiple Paths uses all used paths weighted by their flows (usually more accurate); Gradient descends the least-squares count-error objective — best when you trust the seed matrix. Counts exist on 4 of 6 links (gray links are uncounted — flows there are only pinned down indirectly, which is why the estimate need not equal the hidden truth). Convergence is tracked as %RMSE between assigned volumes and counts. iRelative gap measures how far an assignment is from user equilibrium: gap = (TSTT − SPTT)/TSTT, where TSTT is total travel time at current flows and SPTT is the total if every trip used its current shortest path. At perfect UE nobody can improve → gap = 0. MSA (step 1/k) converges but slowly — it's what SUE uses. Frank-Wolfe adds an optimal line search toward the all-or-nothing solution but zigzags near the optimum. N-conjugate FW (Daneva & Lindberg 2003, Mitradjieva & Lindberg 2013) combines the FW direction with previous direction(s) forced to be Hessian-conjugate — shown here for N=2 (bi-conjugate). It reaches roughly an order of magnitude lower gap in the same iterations, which is why it's TransModeler's most-used method; N>2 can do even better. Path-based methods (Dial's Algorithm B) go further still for very tight gaps (10⁻⁵ and below). The live traffic scene on top makes the iterations physical: vehicles arrive from a negative binomial process (bursty, variance > mean — more realistic than Poisson) at the demand rate, pick a route with probability equal to the current iteration's flow shares, and drive at speeds set by the current route times — press ▶ Play and watch the traffic itself re-distribute as the algorithm hunts for equilibrium.

iStochastic User Equilibrium assumes drivers perceive costs with error: perceived cost = c + ε. The Function you pick in TransModeler's SUE settings is the distribution of ε, and the Error box is its scale θ. With Gumbel errors the route-choice probability has the closed-form logit model — P₁ = 1/(1+e^(−Δc/θ)) — which is why Gumbel is the standard choice. Normal errors give the probit model (no closed form; the S-curve is slightly different in the tails). Uniform errors give a piecewise-linear choice curve that assigns exactly 0 or 1 beyond a finite cost difference — a hard cutoff unlike the other two. Larger θ → flatter curves → more flow on less-attractive routes; as θ→0 all three collapse to deterministic UE. SUE is solved with MSA (provably convergent, slow) — use many iterations. The ringed dot on the chart is the live SUE equilibrium of the two-route example (Route 1: 10 min free-flow, c=1000 · Route 2: 13 min, c=1400): flows shift until Δc and P₁ are consistent. The bar panel compares it to deterministic UE — SUE's signature is the extra flow on the slower route.

Objective: match observed volumes, speeds (and optionally times) — click the 2D map to choose a start point
iA calibration objective surface, the optimization at the heart of ODME: the decision variables are the two route demands (x₁, x₂); the VDF turns them into simulated volumes, speeds and travel times; and the objective F is the mismatch against field observations — a volume term, a speed term, and an optional travel-time term. The observations are deliberately inconsistent (counts from one day, speeds from another — exactly what real calibration data looks like), so with the robust loss (each term saturates, as robust estimators do to resist outliers) the surface has two local optima: one fitting the counts, one fitting the speeds. Frank-Wolfe over the box feasible set picks a corner each iteration (the linear subproblem's answer) and line-searches toward it; N-conjugate blends previous directions. On a non-convex surface FW only finds a stationary point — the default start is deliberately in the pull of the shallower speeds valley, so plain FW gets trapped in a local optimum (the card turns red). The escape tools are the real-world remedies: ⚡ Perturb & retry kicks the converged point randomly and re-runs (sometimes it hops the ridge, sometimes it falls back — that's the honest behavior of perturbation restarts), and 🎲 Multi-start ×8 runs FW from 8 random starts, shows every path, reports how many landed in each valley, and keeps the best — the standard cure for local optima. Click anywhere on the map to try your own start; switch to the squared loss to make the surface single-valley again; drag the 3D surface to rotate and tilt it. Step length λ: by default each iteration does an exact line search — a golden-section minimization of F along the segment from the current point toward the target direction, with λ ≤ 1 so the new point stays feasible (it is a convex combination of feasible points). The alternative rule λ = 2/(k+2) is the classic pre-set diminishing step from FW convergence theory — no search needed, but compare the paths and you'll see why the line search earns its cost.
