iVolume-delay functions map a link's volume/capacity ratio to congested travel time — they are what UE/SUE assignment iterates on. BPR (1964) is the planning workhorse: cheap, flexible via α,β (defaults 0.15, 4; see NCHRP 365) — best for uninterrupted freeway/highway links. Akcelik (HCM 2000) is time-dependent: the analysis-period length T lets it stay finite and meaningful when demand exceeds capacity (X>1) — good for oversaturated corridors and arterials with control delay D₀. Combined link+node (Aashtiani et al.) adds Webster-type signalized and unsignalized intersection delay to a BPR link time — for urban networks where node delay dominates (shown here with a representative Webster form; TransModeler's exact k₁…k₄ formulation follows the same shape). Conical (Spiess 1990) mimics BPR but with bounded derivative and strictly increasing form — numerically better behaved in equilibrium algorithms than high-β BPR. Generalized cost adds tolls and operating cost via a value of time — for toll roads and multi-criteria routing. Logit VDF (Israel ITPR, representative sigmoid) caps runaway times with an S-shape including intersection delay.

Relative gap vs iteration (log scale) on a 3-route network
iRelative gap measures how far an assignment is from user equilibrium: gap = (TSTT − SPTT)/TSTT, where TSTT is total travel time at current flows and SPTT is the total if every trip used its current shortest path. At perfect UE nobody can improve → gap = 0. MSA (step 1/k) converges but slowly — it's what SUE uses. Frank-Wolfe adds an optimal line search toward the all-or-nothing solution but zigzags near the optimum. N-conjugate FW (Daneva & Lindberg 2003, Mitradjieva & Lindberg 2013) combines the FW direction with previous direction(s) forced to be Hessian-conjugate — shown here for N=2 (bi-conjugate). It reaches roughly an order of magnitude lower gap in the same iterations, which is why it's TransModeler's most-used method; N>2 can do even better. Path-based methods (Dial's Algorithm B) go further still for very tight gaps (10⁻⁵ and below). The live traffic scene on top makes the iterations physical: vehicles arrive from a negative binomial process (bursty, variance > mean — more realistic than Poisson) at the demand rate, pick a route with probability equal to the current iteration's flow shares, and drive at speeds set by the current route times — press ▶ Play and watch the traffic itself re-distribute as the algorithm hunts for equilibrium.

P(driver chooses route 1) vs cost difference Δc = c₂ − c₁, for each perception-error distribution
iStochastic User Equilibrium assumes drivers perceive costs with error: perceived cost = c + ε. The Function you pick in TransModeler's SUE settings is the distribution of ε, and the Error box is its scale θ. With Gumbel errors the route-choice probability has the closed-form logit model — P₁ = 1/(1+e^(−Δc/θ)) — which is why Gumbel is the standard choice. Normal errors give the probit model (no closed form; the S-curve is slightly different in the tails). Uniform errors give a piecewise-linear choice curve that assigns exactly 0 or 1 beyond a finite cost difference — a hard cutoff unlike the other two. Larger θ → flatter curves → more flow on less-attractive routes; as θ→0 all three collapse to deterministic UE. SUE is solved with MSA (provably convergent, slow) — use many iterations. The ringed dot on the chart is the live SUE equilibrium of the two-route example (Route 1: 10 min free-flow, c=1000 · Route 2: 13 min, c=1400): flows shift until Δc and P₁ are consistent. The bar panel compares it to deterministic UE — SUE's signature is the extra flow on the slower route.

The Beckmann objective over the feasible flow simplex — every point is a way to split the demand over 3 routes
iTraffic assignment IS constrained optimization: the feasible set of route flows (v₁+v₂+v₃ = demand, vᵢ ≥ 0) is a simplex — the triangle — and UE minimizes the Beckmann objective whose contours are shaded (light = low). Frank-Wolfe is the conditional-gradient method: each iteration solves a linear subproblem over the polytope, whose answer is always a vertex — and in traffic that subproblem is exactly all-or-nothing assignment (the dashed arrow points at the corner "all flow on the cheapest route"). A line search then moves partway along that direction (solid arrow), staying feasible as a convex combination. Near the optimum FW zigzags — successive vertices alternate and steps shrink. N-conjugate FW blends the FW direction with the previous N−1 search directions, forced to be conjugate w.r.t. the Beckmann Hessian: the blended arrow cuts through the zigzag, which is why TransModeler's N-Conjugate UE converges an order of magnitude tighter in the same time. Switch N and replay the exact same problem to see the path straighten.
